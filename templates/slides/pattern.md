The deck presents the sheet in this order:

1. The title slide names the exercise sheet, the problems covered and the course.
2. The roadmap lists the refresher, each problem with its tasks, and the takeaways, and says that every solution follows the same three steps: by hand, algorithm, implementation.
3. The refresher (only if the sheet assumes background students may lack) explains one concept per slide: first the setting, then the definition with its formula, then the key property in words, and finally a common misunderstanding.
4. Then comes each problem in sheet order: first its recap, then each of its tasks.
5. The last slide states the takeaways: one line per lesson of the sheet.

A problem's recap (before its tasks; everything comes from the sheet, nothing is solved yet):

1. The Given slide states what the problem starts from. The first line names the data: where it comes from and what one entry means. The next lines name the model and the formulas the sheet provides, each with its equation number. Then come the functions and results from earlier tasks that this problem builds on. If the sheet shows an image of the data or the setting, it appears next to this list.
2. The overview slide lists the problem's tasks in sheet order, one line each: its number, its short title, and what it asks for in a few words.

A task follows the sheet: first the background the sheet puts before it, then the task itself, then its solution in three steps.

Background (only if the sheet puts background info between the previous task and this one; one slide per idea):
1. Each background slide starts with the question it answers (for example "why this model?"), then gives the sheet's reasoning in short steps, and ends with what this means for the task. Formulas keep the sheet's equation numbers. If the sheet shows an image with this background, it appears on this slide.

What is asked:
2. The task slide restates the task faithfully in the sheet's notation: what must be computed, implemented or shown, with the given starting values, constraints and hints. If the task statement shows an image, it appears here. The last sentence says what the sheet warns about or expects to come out, if it does.

Step 1, by hand:
3. The first by-hand slide works through a toy version small enough to do on paper: it states the toy problem, does each step with the actual numbers, and ends with the subtle point the toy reveals ("Why ...?").
4. The second by-hand slide does the same steps on the real data: a table of the key numbers, then one or two sentences of sanity checks. For a math task, this is the full derivation in numbered steps, with every step explained in words.

Step 2, algorithm:
5. The algorithm slide gives the method as numbered steps in words, from input to output, and ends with the main pitfall.

Step 3, implementation:
6. The implementation slide shows the essential lines of code, the figure next to them if there is one, and one short note per non-obvious line. A pure math task shows the check by computer instead, if the solution has one.
7. The result slide states the final answer in a box, then lists the independent checks that show it is right.
8. If the task has a plot, a plot slide shows it full width with one or two sentences on what to look at.

Finally, if the sheet compares methods or results with an earlier task, a comparison slide puts them side by side in a table and ends with a one-sentence conclusion.

"Think about" questions, mini exercises and overarching questions follow the same steps, shorter.
